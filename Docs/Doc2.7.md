# 2.7 - Imaging System 

Assembling all the previous parts together, and we would then have an imaging system. 

The result of simulating an imaging system have actually been used in previous chapters frequently. Most of the images were generated by the imaging system instead of the single component it was referring to. But here the process shall be further explicated. 


## 2.7.1 - Assembling the System 

We could abstract the system into three major parts: 

- The **object space**. Anything that emits rays can be categorized here. 

- The **lens**. Not necessarily the refractive and reflective surfaces, but anything that alters the direction, position, radiance, or polarization of the ray (as mostly a geometric model, unfortunately we don't have phase here). 

- The **imager**. A special surface that takes all the rays and convert their energy into a visible image. 

Since we know each part has a fixed job, then as long as they abide the contract, then they can be regarded as identical outside its category. External invocation can treat them indifferently, knowing full well that the inner workings does not matter. 

For example, the smallest component in the object space is a point source. In spot grid simulation, that is what is being created - an array of point sources. The flat image source wraps around the point source, as it is nothing but a huge chunk of point sources. Then, an image stack wraps around a single image, as it is just a collection of images. 

From the perspective of the imaging system, we care not what exactly is in the object space. All that is needed is to ask the residents inside the object space to provide ray emissions. 



## 2.7.2 - Restoring Input Brightness 

For the best efficiency and to exploit the Monte Carlo process, every part that can be approximated asymptotically were done that way. 

For example, instead of subscribing the radiance of the ray to the color of their source pixel, we disregard this relationship entirely and use the color only as a probabilistic emission, while every ray it emits have a full radiance. 
In this way, we used the most of the numerical accuracy the computer could provide, while making sure that the resulting value will still be accurate after many Monte Carlo iterations. 

This strategy, however, also brings a problem. 

Assume a pixel of color $(.5, .5, .5)$ is sampled $8$ times during a render run, and $4$ out of the $8$ time did the probabilistic mask decide that it will emit rays. Since each ray carries the fill radiance of $1$, the $4$ successful emission - _assume no vignette and transmission loss_ - left a total radiance of $4$ on the final image, or to day, an RGB value of $(4, 4, 4)$. 

This apparently is wrong, the imaged pixel ended up being $n$ times brighter than the original, where $n$ is iteration count. 

On the surface, this can be mitigated by dividing the sample count, it also happens that we do keep a sample record in the source points and an iteration counter in the render process. But this will not work when the input is a stack of images. 

For a stacked image, a source point being sampled does not mean it will be imaged. It is quite likely that all the rays emitted by the source point will be occluded by other source point in front of it. And since there are so many source points in a stack of images (for a stack consists of only 2 UHD 4K images, there will be $16588800$ source points), a single iteration likely will not be able to sample them all. So the iteration counter also cannot guarantee consistent brightness. 

A combined approach is suggested. 

Assume in each iteration, there are $m$ points in the object space that is being sampled; and there are a total of $n$ points in the object space. Then it is safe to assume that the brightness contributed by a single iteration would be: 

$$p=\frac{m}{n}$$

If over several iterations the sampled number of points equals to the total number of points ($C$ is some constant): 

$$
C\cdot m = n 
$$

At this point the brightness of the resulting image should be the same as the input source. 

This can be used as a basis for normalization. At the end of the rendering, before saving the image, normalize it as: 

$$
I_{out} = \frac{I_{raw}}{C \cdot p}
$$

Or use the definition of $p$:

$$
I_{out} = \frac{I_{raw} \cdot n}{C \cdot m}
$$

Note that even with this normalization, the output may still be a little bit of difference compared to the original. This is often caused by several reasons, the lens will have transmission loss over many surfaces, so it tends to become a bit darker. Samples may not be valid (especially for image stacks), which will also make the result darker. But the brightness of the output will converge and stabilize around a level, which is the more important requirement for post-production.
